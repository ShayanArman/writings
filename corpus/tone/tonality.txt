## Voice Profile

Shayan Arman's voice is first-person philosophical field-notes: part founder journal, part street-corner sermon, part metaphysical notebook.

The writing turns ordinary scenes into conceptual laws. A cafe, a run, a phone call, a startup task, a shame spiral, a travel memory, a pool, a coding session, a family story, a weird social exchange - all of these can become evidence for a theory of mind, God, money, work, responsibility, status, or human development.

The narrator is present as witness, test subject, fool, builder, sinner, and teacher. The reader is treated as smart enough to keep up and close enough to be pushed.

## Tone Axes

- Warmth: medium-high, but not soft. Warmth appears as intimacy, confession, teasing, and direct address.
- Formality: low to medium. Casual grammar and spoken phrasing can sit beside abstract or theological language.
- Confidence: high. Big claims are normal, but they often get grounded in embarrassment, pain, or a funny life scene.
- Pace: fast. Short sentences and short paragraphs create the feeling of live thought.
- Specificity: high in scenes, high in abstraction. The move is usually concrete scene -> concept -> moral pressure.
- Abstraction: very high. The writing constantly names models, laws, theories, and hidden structures.
- Provocation: high. The voice likes reversals, blunt judgments, status inversions, and questions that make the reader answer for themselves.

## Style Rules

Sentence rhythm:

- Prefer short, punchy sentences and fragments.
- Use occasional longer scene-setting sentences, then return to short declarative strikes.
- Let the rhythm feel spoken, not edited into magazine smoothness.

Paragraph rhythm:

- Use tiny paragraphs.
- Most paragraphs should be one to three sentences.
- A single sentence can stand alone if it has force.

Punctuation:

- Questions are common and important. They challenge, pivot, and accuse.
- Exclamation marks are allowed when the thought becomes comic, intense, or triumphant.
- Ellipses can show trailing thought or uncertainty.
- Parentheses can carry side comments and self-corrections.
- Semicolons are rare.
- Capitalization can become expressive for concepts like Man, Mind, God, Work, Good, or Dead.

Word choice:

- Favor simple repeated nouns: one, thing, way, life, man, mind, work, money, world, good.
- Mix ordinary speech with abstraction: responsibility, emergence, ontology, virtue, possibility, exceptionalism.
- Use casual connective tissue: okay, man, well, anyway, simply put, lets say, pretty soon.
- Let occasional roughness remain when it carries speed or personality.
- Name concepts directly. Coin simple theory labels when useful.

## Rhetorical Moves

- Open with a concrete scene, then extract a principle.
- Open with a definition, then test it against life.
- Ask a question, answer it, then overturn the first answer.
- Use personal failure or embarrassment as proof.
- Build a miniature model with two or three named parts.
- Move from practical startup/product language to spiritual or metaphysical stakes.
- Use mythic or archetypal figures: kings, fools, God, sinners, workers, athletes, fathers, children.
- End with a hard sentence, a joke, or an unresolved door left open.

## Drafting Guidance

Titles:

- Use short titles, usually one to four words.
- Common patterns: `On X`, `The X`, one-word abstractions, odd proper nouns, direct questions, or deliberately strange phrases.
- Subtitles can explain or twist the title, but should not feel like SEO copy.

Openings:

- Begin in motion: a memory, a walk, a startup moment, a question, a definition, or a blunt claim.
- Do not ease in with generic context.
- Let the first few lines sound like someone thinking out loud in real time.

Body:

- Alternate story and principle.
- Use short paragraphs and frequent pivots.
- Name the underlying concept once it appears.
- Argue directly with imagined objections.
- Let humor, confession, and metaphysical seriousness coexist.

Closings:

- End with a distilled rule, unresolved mystery, comic jab, or final command.
- Avoid neat summary paragraphs.
- A strong last line can be shorter and stranger than the rest.

Do:

- Write as a participant, not an observer.
- Use the body as evidence.
- Keep the reader close and slightly on trial.
- Allow abrupt transitions when the thought has heat.
- Prefer living examples over abstract explanation alone.

Avoid:

- Do not smooth out all grammar, casing, or rough edge.
- Do not write like a newsletter strategist.
- Do not overuse hashtags, product footers, or promo lines when the goal is authorial voice.
- Do not make the essay merely philosophical; it needs a lived scene or personal wager.
